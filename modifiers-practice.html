Modifiers

Monica Winkler  |  Digital SAT English Prep

What is a modifier?

A modifier is an extra word, phrase, or clause that gives us more information about something else in the sentence.

The SAT tests one key idea: a modifier must be placed next to the word it modifies.

Your two jobs on every question:

  • Identify and label the modifier.
  • Identify what it is modifying (or supposed to be modifying).

What does a modifier look like?

Modifiers can be single words, prepositional phrases, participial phrases, or clauses

Consider: The cats in the cat café were so cute!

Here, "in the cat café" is a modifier: it tells us more about the cats. Because it starts with the preposition in, it's called a prepositional phrase.

Common modifier types the SAT uses:

  • Prepositional phrases: in the lab, after the meeting, with great care
  • Participial phrases: running across the field, having studied all night, exhausted by the climb
  • Infinitive phrases: to finish the report, to earn her degree
SAT tip: On the test, the modifier always appears before the blank. Your job is to make sure the subject that fills the blank is the one performing the action in that opening phrase.

Dangling Modifiers

The implied subject of the opening phrase must match the grammatical subject of the sentence

A dangling modifier is an opening phrase whose implied subject doesn't match the actual subject of the main clause. The phrase is "dangling", as it has nothing to properly attach to.

The SAT's most common dangling modifier structure:

Opening participial phrase, ________.

The blank must be filled so that the subject of the main clause is the one performing the action in the opening phrase.

✗ Dangling
After actively working on her thesis for years, relief was the main thing Mary felt.
✓ Fixed
After actively working on her thesis for years, Mary was relieved to finally be finished.
SAT tip: As soon as you see a sentence that opens with a comma-framed phrase, ask: Who is doing the action in this phrase? That person (or thing) must be the subject right after the comma. If it isn't, it's a dangling modifier.

Misplaced Modifiers

A modifier placed too far from what it describes creates ambiguity or absurdity

A misplaced modifier is one that has technically attached to something, but to the wrong thing, because it's in the wrong spot. The result is often unintentionally funny or confusing.

✗ Misplaced
Flying over the mountains, the village below looked picturesque.

The village isn't flying, but grammatically, it is.
✓ Fixed
Flying over the mountains, the passengers looked at the picturesque village below.
Quick test for dangling vs. misplaced: If the phrase has nothing grammatically correct to attach to → dangling. If it has attached to the wrong thing → misplaced. Both are fixed by restructuring so the modifier sits next to what it describes.
