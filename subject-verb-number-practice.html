Subject-Verb Agreement: Number

Monica Winkler  |  Digital SAT English Prep
Step 1: Label the Subject and Main Verb

What is a subject?

The subject is the who or what the sentence is about: the main noun.

What is a main verb?

The main verb is either an action (e.g., talk, walk, baked) or a linking element (e.g., is, are, was, were).

Find the subject by asking: Who or what is doing the action (or being described)?

Yesterday my really nice and lovely grandma baked me some cookies.
Who baked? Grandma. Subject: grandma. Main verb: baked.

How to spot a subject-verb agreement question

Look at the answer choices. They will all be the same verb, but some are singular and some are plural. Label each choice with it (singular) or they (plural):

A.runsit
B.runthey
C.is runningit
D.have runit

Three choices are singular (it) and one is plural (they). When you see this pattern, the question is testing subject-verb agreement.

Singular (it)
runs, walks, is, was, has, bakes
Plural (they)
run, walk, are, were, have, bake
The rule: A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a plural verb. If you can replace the subject with it, use the singular form. If you can replace it with they, use the plural form.
Step 2: Learn to Ignore the Distractors

Prepositional phrases are extra information: cross them out

A prepositional phrase begins with a preposition (words like of, in, from, at, by, about, with, between) and does not contain the main subject. The SAT places these phrases between the subject and the verb to make you lose track of the subject. Cross them out.

✕ from down the street ✕ in downtown ✕ with all its dog friends ✕ about the dog
Example:
The book about the dog with all its dog friends came out.
About and with are both prepositions. Cross out both phrases they introduce:

The book about the dog with all its dog friends came out.

Subject: book (singular). Verb: came (singular). Correct.

Information between commas or dashes: cross it out too

When extra information is sandwiched between two commas or between two dashes, remove the whole chunk. What remains is the core sentence.

Commas:
Mary, who I met last week, is so nice.
Cross it out: Mary, who I met last week, is so nice.
Subject: Mary (singular). Verb: is. Correct.

Dashes:
The terribly seasoned chicken, the one I told you about, tastes so bad.
Cross it out: The terribly seasoned chicken, the one I told you about, tastes so bad.
Subject: chicken (singular). Verb: tastes. Correct.

"That" starts a dependent clause with its own subject

When you see the word that, it introduces a dependent clause with its own subject and verb. If the blank is inside the dependent clause, its subject is the noun inside that clause, not the noun that comes before that.

Example:

The scientist proposed the idea that the techniques researchers _____ the results.

The word that introduces a dependent clause: that the techniques researchers _____ the results. The blank is the verb of this clause. Its subject is techniques (plural), which is inside the clause, right after that.

The noun before that is idea (singular), but that is the subject of the main verb (proposed), not of the blank.

Correct verb for the blank: shape (plural, to match techniques).
The rule: When the blank is inside a "that" clause, find the subject of that clause. It will be the noun right after that, not the noun before it.

The odd one out trick

Once you have labeled each choice with it or they, one choice will always be the odd one out: three choices will share the same pronoun and one will be different. That odd one out is always the answer.

A.runsit
B.runthey ← odd one out
C.is runningit
D.has runit

B is the only plural choice. It is the odd one out, so the subject must be plural. You do not even need to check: B is the answer.

Why this works: The SAT always uses three choices of one type and one of the other. So the moment you label all four, the answer reveals itself. Use this as a shortcut to confirm your work once you have identified the subject.

Put it all together: the strategy

  • 1 Notice the answer choices are all the same verb in singular or plural form.
  • 2 Find the blank and look left for the subject.
  • 3 Cross out any prepositional phrases, comma-sandwiched chunks, or dash-sandwiched chunks between the subject and the blank.
  • 4 If you see that, find the subject of the clause that follows it.
  • 5 Ask: can I replace the subject with it or they? Choose the matching verb form.
  • 6 Label all four choices with it or they. The odd one out is the answer.
