Colon Rules & Practice

Rule 1
Introducing a list
Use a colon after a complete sentence that introduces a list. The clause before the colon must be able to stand alone.
Correct
She packed three things: a map, a compass, and a flashlight.
Incorrect
She packed: a map, a compass, and a flashlight.
Rule 2
Introducing an explanation or elaboration
Use a colon after a complete clause to signal that what follows explains or expands on the idea just stated.
Correct
The meeting was a success: every team agreed on the new deadline.
Incorrect
The reason was: they were tired.
Rule 3
Introducing a quotation
A colon can introduce a formal or long quotation, especially when the lead-in is a complete sentence.
Correct
Lincoln's words still resonate: "Four score and seven years ago…"
Rule 4
Between independent clauses
A colon can join two independent clauses when the second one directly explains or illustrates the first.
Correct
I know why she left: the job never challenged her.
Rule 5
Capitalization after a colon
If what follows the colon is a complete sentence, you may capitalize the first word (both styles are accepted). If it's a phrase or list, keep it lowercase.
Both accepted
She had one goal: Win the championship. or win the championship.
Lowercase for phrases
She brought two things: her notebook and her pen.
Rule 6
Do NOT use a colon after a verb or preposition
Never place a colon directly after a verb, preposition, or "such as" / "including," as the sentence is not yet complete at that point.
Correct
The countries include France, Italy, and Spain.
Incorrect
The countries include: France, Italy, and Spain.
