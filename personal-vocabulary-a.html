Group 1 of 5

Taking a Position

These words describe how someone stands in relation to a belief, opinion, or outcome. Do they commit, dodge, shout, or stay calm?

Pair 1 — do you commit, or dodge?
Endorse verb in + dorsum
To publicly declare support for something or someone.

Root breakdown

in (on) + dorsum (back, Latin). To put your name on the back of something. Banks stamped the backs of checks to approve them.

Word family

dorsal fin (on the back)endorsement

Example

"The senator endorsed the climate bill, saying it had her full support."

A dolphin's dorsal fin is on its back. When you endorse something, you carry it on your back — publicly.
vs.
Equivocate verb aequi + vox
To speak ambiguously to avoid committing to a position.

Root breakdown

aequi (equal) + vox/vocis (voice, Latin). Two equal voices, both canceling each other out. You say everything and nothing at once.

Word family

equivocalvocalvoice

Example

"When asked if he liked the plan, he equivocated: 'Well, there are certainly aspects worth considering...'"

Politicians equivocate when they don't want to lose any voters. Both sides hear what they want.
Pair 2 — confident, but loud or calm?
Vociferous adjective vox + ferre
Expressing opinions loudly and forcefully; hard to ignore.

Root breakdown

vox/vocis (voice) + ferre (to carry, Latin). Carrying your voice everywhere, at full volume.

Word family

vocaladvocateprovokerevokevocation

Example

"The crowd was vociferous in its opposition, chanting and shouting throughout the speech."

Vociferous people are like megaphones. They do not equivocate — you cannot miss them.
vs.
Sanguine adjective sanguis
Confidently optimistic, especially in a difficult situation.

Root breakdown

sanguis (blood, Latin). From medieval medicine: people with warm, blood-rich temperaments were considered naturally cheerful and optimistic.

Word family

sanguinary (bloody)consanguine (related by blood)

Example

"Despite the setbacks, she remained sanguine, confident the project would succeed in the end."

Unlike vociferous people who shout, sanguine people smile quietly and say "it'll work out."

Shared root: vox (voice)

  • equivocate — two equal voices
  • vociferous — carrying the voice
  • advocate — speaking for
  • provoke — calling out
  • revoke — calling back

Group 1 self-check

Q1

A coach stays calm and optimistic even though the team is losing badly. Which word fits?

Sanguine. The coach is quietly confident despite difficulty, not panicked or loud.
Q2

A politician is asked "Do you support the new tax?" and replies: "There are many perspectives to consider, and I think it's important to look at all sides carefully." What are they doing?

Equivocating. Deliberately vague to avoid committing to yes or no.
Q3

Vociferous and sanguine both describe someone with a strong position. What is the key difference?

Volume and tone. Vociferous = loud, forceful, impossible to ignore. Sanguine = calm, quietly optimistic. Both believe in their position; they express it very differently.
Group 2 of 5

Changing or Affecting Things

These four words are all about actions that alter a situation: softening it, replacing it, adding to it, or taking away from it.

Pair 1 — softening vs. replacing
Mitigate verb mitis + agere
To make something less severe, harmful, or painful.

Root breakdown

mitis (mild, gentle) + agere (to do/act, Latin). To act to make something milder. You don't eliminate the problem — you reduce it.

Word family

mitigationmitigating circumstances

Example

"Wearing sunscreen mitigates the risk of sunburn on long hikes."

Think "make it mild." Mitigate doesn't remove the danger — it turns it down.
vs.
Supersede verb super + sedere
To take the place of something, making it outdated or no longer in use.

Root breakdown

super (above) + sedere (to sit, Latin). To sit above something — and push it out. Note the spelling: supersede, not "supercede."

Word family

presideresidesedentarysession

Example

"Streaming services have largely superseded DVD rentals."

Super (above) + sit. The new thing sits above the old thing and replaces it entirely. Mitigate softens; supersede replaces.
Pair 2 — creating something vs. taking something away
Engender verb en + genus
To cause or give rise to a feeling, situation, or condition.

Root breakdown

en (cause to be) + genus/generare (to beget, produce, Latin). To bring something into being. Often used for abstract things: trust, conflict, fear.

Word family

generategendergenregeneral

Example

"The principal's open-door policy engendered trust among students."

Engender = generate. You are producing something new in the world — usually a feeling or situation.
vs.
Detract verb de + trahere
To reduce the quality, value, or importance of something.

Root breakdown

de (away) + trahere (to pull, Latin). To pull value away from something. You don't destroy it — you diminish it.

Word family

distractextractattracttractor

Example

"The spelling errors detracted from an otherwise strong essay."

The tract root means pull. De-tract = pull away. Engender adds; detract subtracts.

Shared root: trahere (to pull)

  • detract — pull away
  • distract — pull attention away
  • attract — pull toward
  • extract — pull out
  • contract — pull together
  • retract — pull back

Group 2 self-check

Q1

New safety regulations have replaced the old ones and made them no longer valid. Which word fits?

Supersede. The new regulations sit above and replace the old ones entirely.
Q2

A loud argument at a dinner party ruins the warm atmosphere that had been building all evening. What did the argument do to the atmosphere, and what did the evening do before that?

The warm evening engendered a good atmosphere (created/gave rise to it). The argument detracted from it (pulled away its value).
Q3

What is the difference between mitigating a problem and superseding it?

Mitigate = reduce the harm, but the problem still exists. Supersede = the old thing is replaced entirely by something new. One softens; the other replaces.
Group 3 of 5

Language and Information

These words all relate to how we handle information: what a word points to, how we compress it, how we judge it, and how we organize it.

Pair 1 — what a word means vs. a summary of what something means
Denote verb de + notare
To be a sign of something; to indicate or represent directly.

Root breakdown

de (down, fully) + notare (to mark, Latin). To mark something down clearly. The literal, direct meaning of a word or symbol.

Word family

notenotationannotateconnote (implied meaning)

Example

"The red traffic light denotes 'stop' in most countries."

De-note = mark it down directly. The dictionary definition is what a word denotes. What it makes you feel is what it connotes.
vs.
Synopsis noun syn + opsis
A brief summary or overview of a longer work or set of events.

Root breakdown

syn (together) + opsis (seeing, Greek). To see everything together at once — the whole story compressed into a single view.

Word family

opticopticalsynopticsynchronize

Example

"The back cover of the novel gave a synopsis of the plot without revealing the ending."

Think of a camera pulling back to see the whole picture at once. A synopsis = the bird's eye view.
Pair 2 — evaluating vs. organizing
Appraising verb (present participle) ad + pretium
Assessing the value, quality, or nature of something; evaluating carefully.

Root breakdown

ad (to) + pretium (price/value, Latin). To assign a price or value to something.

Word family

appraiseappraisalappreciateprecious

Example

"She stood appraising the painting for a long time before making an offer."

Common confusion: Appraise (evaluate) vs. apprise (inform). "I appraised the car's value" vs. "I apprised him of the situation."
vs.
Collate verb com + latus
To gather, arrange, and compare information or documents in an organized way.

Root breakdown

com (together) + latus (brought, Latin). To bring things together for comparison.

Word family

collationcollectcorrelate

Example

"The researcher collated data from 40 different studies before drawing any conclusions."

Appraising = judging what something is worth. Collating = gathering and sorting the material so you can judge it. Collating comes first.

Group 3 self-check

Q1

A real estate agent visits a house and carefully evaluates it before setting a price. Which word fits?

Appraising. The agent is assessing value — literally the origin of the word.
Q2

A student gathers notes from six different sources and organizes them before writing an essay. What are they doing to the notes?

Collating. Bringing together and organizing material from multiple sources.
Q3

What does the word "synopsis" literally mean, based on its roots?

Seeing everything together at once (syn = together, opsis = seeing). A synopsis compresses a whole work into one view.
Group 4 of 5

Conscription

This word stands on its own. It has a rich root and a precise meaning worth knowing well.

Conscription noun com + scribere
The mandatory enrollment of people into national military service; the draft.

Root breakdown

com (together) + scribere (to write, Latin). Originally: to write names together on a list for military service. The state writes your name down — and you have no choice.

Word family

scriptdescribeprescribeinscriptionmanuscriptsubscribe

Example

"During the war, conscription was introduced because not enough soldiers had volunteered."

Key contrast

Conscription is the opposite of voluntary enlistment. If you volunteer, you choose. If you are conscripted, the government chooses for you.

The script root = writing. Con-scription = co-writing your name onto a list you didn't sign yourself.

Shared root: scribere (to write)

  • conscription — writing names onto a list
  • describe — write down
  • prescribe — write before (a rule)
  • subscribe — write under (agree to)
  • transcribe — write across (copy)
  • manuscript — hand-written
  • inscription — written into

Group 4 self-check

Q1

What is the key difference between conscription and voluntary enlistment?

Conscription is mandatory — the state requires you to serve. Voluntary enlistment is a personal choice. Conscription removes the choice.
Q2

Based on its root (scribere = to write), explain in your own words why "conscription" makes sense as a word for forced military service.

The government "writes your name together" with others on a military list. You didn't sign it yourself — it was written for you. The root captures the idea of being registered or recorded without consent.
Group 5 of 5

Society and Behavior

These words describe how people relate to authority, how they react to surprising information, how conditions change over time, and how groups grow or claim meaning. Together they map the social world.

Pair 1 — respect and connection: how do you relate to others?
Deferential adjective de + ferre
Showing humble respect and willingness to yield to another's judgment or authority.

Root breakdown

de (down, away) + ferre (to carry, Latin). To carry yourself downward in the presence of another — to step back and let them lead. The same ferre root appears in vociferous (carrying the voice) and transfer (carry across).

Word family

deferdeferencetransferconferrefer

Example

"The new employee was deferential toward the senior staff, always asking for guidance rather than acting on her own."

Deferential people defer — they step back. They don't push back or take over. They yield.
vs.
Rapport noun re + apporter
A close, harmonious relationship built on mutual understanding and trust.

Root breakdown

re (back) + apporter (to bring, Old French). To bring things back together — to create a connection by returning to each other. Pronounced ra-POR; the final t is silent.

Word family

rapport (no close English relatives)report (bring back news)

Example

"The teacher built strong rapport with her students by remembering their names and asking about their interests."

Deferential is about hierarchy — one person steps back for another. Rapport is about equality — two people meet in the middle and connect.
Pair 2 — disbelief and doubt
Incredulous adjective in + credere
Unable or unwilling to believe something; showing disbelief.

Root breakdown

in (not) + credere (to believe, Latin). Not believing. The same root gives us incredible (unbelievable), credit (trust), and creed (what one believes).

Word family

incrediblecrediblecreditcreedcredence

Example

"She was incredulous when she heard the price — she stared at the receipt and asked the cashier to check again."

Key distinction

Incredulous describes the person who doesn't believe. Incredible describes the thing that is hard to believe. "She was incredulous about the incredible story."

Incredulous face = raised eyebrows, open mouth, disbelief. You've just heard something you cannot credit as true.
Pair 3 — improvement and growth: bouncing back vs. spreading out
Remission noun re + mittere
A reduction or disappearance of symptoms, especially of a disease; also, the cancellation of a debt or punishment.

Root breakdown

re (back) + mittere (to send, Latin). To send back — to return something (the illness, the debt, the sentence) to a lesser or absent state. The same root gives us mission, dismiss, and submit.

Word family

missiondismisssubmitemitpermittransmit

Example

"After two years of treatment, the cancer went into remission and her doctors were cautiously optimistic."

Re-mission = the disease is "sent back." It hasn't disappeared for good — it has retreated. Remission is hopeful, but not the same as cured.
vs.
Proliferate verb proles + ferre
To increase rapidly in number; to spread or multiply quickly.

Root breakdown

proles (offspring, Latin) + ferre (to bear/carry). To carry offspring — to keep producing new generations. The same ferre root appears in deferential and vociferous.

Word family

proliferationproletariat (from proles)

Example

"Social media platforms proliferated rapidly in the 2010s, with dozens of new apps launching every year."

Remission = pulling back. Proliferate = pushing outward. One shrinks; the other multiplies.
Pair 4 — meaning and money: what does it claim vs. what does it cost?
Purport verb / noun pur + porter
To appear or claim to be something, often without proof; also, the meaning or intention of something.

Root breakdown

pur/pro (forth) + porter (to carry, Old French). To carry something forward as a claim — to put a meaning out into the world, whether or not it is true.

Word family

purportedpurportedlyimport (carry in)export (carry out)

Example

"The document purported to be a letter from the governor, but the signature looked suspicious."

As a noun

"The purport of the new policy was to reduce wait times, but critics said it would do the opposite."

Purport carries a claim out into the world. It doesn't promise the claim is true — just that someone is making it.
vs.
Austerity noun austerus
Deliberate strictness and self-denial; in economics, government spending cuts and reduced services to lower debt.

Root breakdown

austerus (harsh, dry, bitter, Latin/Greek). Originally described dry, bitter-tasting things. The meaning shifted to describe harsh, stripped-down conditions — personal or economic.

Word family

austereausterely

Example

"The government announced a period of austerity, cutting funding for public programs to address the national deficit."

Two uses

Personal: an austere lifestyle (bare, no luxuries). Economic: austerity measures (spending cuts, reduced services). Both share the core meaning of deliberate hardship.

Purport is about what something claims to be. Austerity is about what is actually being stripped away. One is about appearance; the other is about reality.

Shared root: ferre (to carry/bear)

  • deferential — carry yourself downward (yield)
  • vociferous — carry the voice (be loud)
  • proliferate — carry offspring (multiply)
  • transfer — carry across
  • confer — carry together (discuss)
  • refer — carry back

Group 5 self-check

Q1

A student receives news that feels impossible to believe — her face shows total disbelief. Which word fits?

Incredulous. She is the person who cannot believe it. (The news itself might be incredible — but the word for her reaction is incredulous.)
Q2

A new intern always asks senior colleagues for their input before making decisions and never pushes her own agenda. How would you describe her manner?

Deferential. She consistently yields to others' judgment — she carries herself downward in their presence.
Q3

What is the difference between remission and proliferation? Use a medical context.

Remission = the disease retreats or symptoms decrease. Proliferation = cells multiply rapidly (often used to describe cancer spreading). They are nearly opposites: one pulls back, the other expands.
Q4

A leaked memo purports to show that the company knew about the defect for years. What does "purports" signal here?

That the memo claims or appears to show this — but the writer is not confirming it is genuine or true. Purport carries the claim without vouching for it.
Q5

How are rapport and deferential different in how they describe relationships?

Rapport = mutual, equal connection built on trust. Deferential = one-sided yielding to someone with more authority. Rapport is horizontal; deferential is vertical.

Q1 — Definition match

Which word means "to make something less severe without eliminating it"?

Mitigate.
Q2 — Scenario

A famous chef gives a glowing public recommendation for a new kitchen appliance. Which word fits?

Endorse. The chef is publicly backing the product.
Q3 — Roots

Three words in this list share the Latin root ferre (to carry). Name them.

Vociferous, deferential, and proliferate. All involve carrying: carrying the voice, carrying yourself downward, carrying offspring.
Q4 — Contrast

What is the difference between engender and detract? Use a single example situation to show both.

Engender = bring something into existence. Detract = reduce or take away from something. Example: a kind teacher engenders confidence in students; harsh criticism detracts from their motivation.
Q5 — Sentence writing

Write a sentence using "supersede" correctly.

Example: "The updated guidelines superseded the 2019 version, making it officially outdated." Key: something old is replaced entirely by something new.
Q6 — Scenario

A librarian is gathering articles from 12 journals, putting them in date order, and grouping them by topic. Which word describes what she is doing?

Collating. She is organizing and bringing together material from multiple sources.
Q7 — Precision

What does "denote" mean, and how is it different from "connote"?

Denote = the direct, literal meaning. Connote = the implied or emotional meaning. "Snake" denotes a reptile. It connotes danger or deception.
Q8 — Scenario

A country passes a law requiring all 18-year-olds to serve one year in the military. What is this policy called?

Conscription. It is mandatory, not voluntary.
Q9 — New words: reaction

A journalist reads a report claiming that a trusted official has been lying for years. Her expression shows she simply cannot believe it. Which word describes her reaction?

Incredulous. She is the person who cannot believe it — her disbelief is the focus.
Q10 — New words: society

Use remission, proliferate, austerity, and rapport in a short paragraph of 4 to 6 sentences on any topic.

Model: "During the years of austerity that followed the financial crisis, public services were cut and communities struggled. Yet in some neighborhoods, a surprising sense of rapport emerged — people who had never spoken began sharing resources and looking out for each other. Food banks proliferated across the city, turning empty storefronts into distribution centers overnight. For many families, this informal network felt like a remission of the worst pressures, even as the underlying problems remained."
Q11 — New words: purport vs. denote

How is "purport" different from "denote"? Both involve meaning — what is the key distinction?

Denote = the direct, established meaning of a word or symbol (certain). Purport = what something claims or appears to mean, without confirming it is true (uncertain). A red light denotes "stop." A forged document purports to be official.
