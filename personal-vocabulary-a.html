Group 1 of 4

Taking a Position

These words describe how someone stands in relation to a belief, opinion, or outcome. Do they commit, dodge, shout, or stay calm?

Pair 1 — do you commit, or dodge?

Endorse
verb
in + dorsum
To publicly declare support for something or someone.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
in (on) + dorsum (back, Latin). To put your name on the back of something. Banks stamped the backs of checks to approve them.
Word family
dorsal fin (on the back) endorsement
Example
"The senator endorsed the climate bill, saying it had her full support."
Memory trick
A dolphin's dorsal fin is on its back. When you endorse something, you carry it on your back — publicly.
vs.
Equivocate
verb
aequi + vox
To speak ambiguously to avoid committing to a position.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
aequi (equal) + vox/vocis (voice, Latin). Two equal voices, both canceling each other out. You say everything and nothing at once.
Word family
equivocal vocal voice
Example
"When asked if he liked the plan, he equivocated: 'Well, there are certainly aspects worth considering...'"
Memory trick
Politicians equivocate when they don't want to lose any voters. Both sides hear what they want.

Pair 2 — confident, but loud or calm?

Vociferous
adjective
vox + ferre
Expressing opinions loudly and forcefully; hard to ignore.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
vox/vocis (voice) + ferre (to carry, Latin). Carrying your voice everywhere, at full volume. Same vox root as equivocate — but no ambiguity here.
Word family
vocal advocate provoke revoke vocation
Example
"The crowd was vociferous in its opposition, chanting and shouting throughout the speech."
Memory trick
Vociferous people are like megaphones. They do not equivocate — you cannot miss them.
vs.
Sanguine
adjective
sanguis
Confidently optimistic, especially in a difficult situation.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
sanguis (blood, Latin). From medieval medicine: people with warm, blood-rich temperaments were considered naturally cheerful and optimistic.
Word family
sanguinary (bloody) consanguine (related by blood)
Example
"Despite the setbacks, she remained sanguine, confident the project would succeed in the end."
Memory trick
Unlike vociferous people who shout, sanguine people smile quietly and say "it'll work out."

Shared root: vox (voice)

Three of these four words share the Latin root vox/vocis. Recognizing it helps you decode unfamiliar words for life.

equivocate — two equal voices vociferous — carrying the voice advocate — speaking for provoke — calling out revoke — calling back

Group 1 self-check

Write your answer, then reveal to compare.

Q1
A coach stays calm and optimistic even though the team is losing badly. Which word fits?
Sanguine. The coach is quietly confident despite difficulty, not panicked or loud.
Q2
A politician is asked "Do you support the new tax?" and replies: "There are many perspectives to consider, and I think it's important to look at all sides carefully." What are they doing?
Equivocating. Deliberately vague to avoid committing to yes or no.
Q3
Vociferous and sanguine both describe someone with a strong position. What is the key difference?
Volume and tone. Vociferous = loud, forceful, impossible to ignore. Sanguine = calm, quietly optimistic. Both believe in their position; they express it very differently.

Group 2 of 4

Changing or Affecting Things

These four words are all about actions that alter a situation: softening it, replacing it, adding to it, or taking away from it.

Pair 1 — softening vs. replacing

Mitigate
verb
mitis + agere
To make something less severe, harmful, or painful.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
mitis (mild, gentle) + agere (to do/act, Latin). To act to make something milder. You don't eliminate the problem — you reduce it.
Word family
mitigation mitigating circumstances
Example
"Wearing sunscreen mitigates the risk of sunburn on long hikes."
Memory trick
Think "make it mild." Mitigate doesn't remove the danger — it turns it down.
vs.
Supersede
verb
super + sedere
To take the place of something, making it outdated or no longer in use.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
super (above) + sedere (to sit, Latin). To sit above something — and push it out. Note the spelling: supersede, not "supercede." One of the most commonly misspelled words in English.
Word family
preside (sit before) reside (sit back) sedentary (sitting still) session
Example
"Streaming services have largely superseded DVD rentals."
Memory trick
Super (above) + sit. The new thing sits above the old thing and replaces it entirely. Mitigate softens; supersede replaces.

Pair 2 — creating something vs. taking something away

Engender
verb
en + genus
To cause or give rise to a feeling, situation, or condition.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
en (cause to be) + genus/generare (to beget, produce, Latin). To bring something into being. Often used for abstract things: trust, conflict, fear.
Word family
generate gender (originally: kind/type) genre general
Example
"The principal's open-door policy engendered trust among students."
Memory trick
Engender = generate. You are producing something new in the world — usually a feeling or situation.
vs.
Detract
verb
de + trahere
To reduce the quality, value, or importance of something.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
de (away) + trahere (to pull, Latin). To pull value away from something. You don't destroy it — you diminish it.
Word family
distract (pull attention away) extract (pull out) attract (pull toward) tractor
Example
"The spelling errors detracted from an otherwise strong essay."
Memory trick
The tract root means pull. De-tract = pull away. Engender adds; detract subtracts.

Shared root: trahere (to pull)

Detract shares the tract root with several common words. Once you see it, you'll spot it everywhere.

detract — pull away distract — pull attention away attract — pull toward extract — pull out contract — pull together retract — pull back

Group 2 self-check

Write your answer, then reveal to compare.

Q1
New safety regulations have replaced the old ones and made them no longer valid. Which word fits?
Supersede. The new regulations sit above and replace the old ones entirely.
Q2
A loud argument at a dinner party ruins the warm atmosphere that had been building all evening. What did the argument do to the atmosphere, and what did the evening do before that?
The warm evening engendered a good atmosphere (created/gave rise to it). The argument detracted from it (pulled away its value).
Q3
What is the difference between mitigating a problem and superseding it?
Mitigate = reduce the harm, but the problem still exists. Supersede = the old thing is replaced entirely by something new. One softens; the other replaces.

Group 3 of 4

Language and Information

These four words all relate to how we handle information: what a word points to, how we compress it, how we judge it, and how we organize it.

Pair 1 — what a word means vs. a summary of what something means

Denote
verb
de + notare
To be a sign of something; to indicate or represent directly.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
de (down, fully) + notare (to mark, Latin). To mark something down clearly. Denote is the literal, direct meaning of a word or symbol, as opposed to its emotional associations.
Word family
note notation annotate connote (implied meaning)
Example
"The red traffic light denotes 'stop' in most countries."
Memory trick
De-note = mark it down directly. The dictionary definition of a word is what it denotes. What it makes you feel is what it connotes.
vs.
Synopsis
noun
syn + opsis
A brief summary or overview of a longer work or set of events.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
syn (together) + opsis (seeing, Greek). To see everything together at once — the whole story compressed into a single view.
Word family
optic (eye) optical synopsis — synoptic synchronize (syn = together)
Example
"The back cover of the novel gave a synopsis of the plot without revealing the ending."
Memory trick
Think of a camera pulling back to see the whole picture at once. A synopsis = the bird's eye view.

Pair 2 — evaluating vs. organizing

Appraising
verb (present participle)
ad + pretium
Assessing the value, quality, or nature of something; evaluating carefully.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
ad (to) + pretium (price/value, Latin). To assign a price or value to something. Originally used for valuing property; now used for any kind of careful evaluation.
Word family
appraise appraisal appreciate (recognize the value) precious (of high price)
Example
"She stood appraising the painting for a long time before making an offer."
Common confusion
Appraise (evaluate) vs. apprise (inform). "I appraised the car's value" vs. "I apprised him of the situation."
vs.
Collate
verb
com + latus
To gather, arrange, and compare information or documents in an organized way.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
com (together) + latus (brought, Latin, from ferre). To bring things together for comparison. A printer "collates" pages — assembles them in the right order.
Word family
collation collect (bring together) correlate (bring together + relate)
Example
"The researcher collated data from 40 different studies before drawing any conclusions."
Memory trick
Appraising = judging what something is worth. Collating = gathering and sorting the material so you can judge it. Collating comes first.

Group 3 self-check

Write your answer, then reveal to compare.

Q1
A real estate agent visits a house and carefully evaluates it before setting a price. Which word fits?
Appraising. The agent is assessing value — literally the origin of the word.
Q2
A student gathers notes from six different sources and organizes them before writing an essay. What are they doing to the notes?
Collating. Bringing together and organizing material from multiple sources.
Q3
What does the word "synopsis" literally mean, based on its roots?
Seeing everything together at once (syn = together, opsis = seeing). A synopsis compresses a whole work into one view.

Group 4 of 4

Conscription

This word stands on its own. It has a rich root and a precise meaning worth knowing well.

Conscription
noun
com + scribere
The mandatory enrollment of people into a national military service; the draft.
tap for roots and examples ▾
Root breakdown
com (together) + scribere (to write, Latin). Originally: to write names together on a list for military service. The state writes your name down — and you have no choice.
Word family
script (written) describe (write down) prescribe inscription manuscript (written by hand) subscribe (write under)
Example
"During the war, conscription was introduced because not enough soldiers had volunteered."
Key contrast
Conscription is the opposite of voluntary enlistment. If you volunteer, you choose. If you are conscripted, the government chooses for you.
Memory trick
The script root = writing. Con-scription = co-writing your name onto a list you didn't sign yourself.

Shared root: scribere (to write)

The scrib/script root is one of the most productive in English. Once you see it, you'll find it in dozens of words.

conscription — writing names onto a list describe — write down prescribe — write before (a rule) subscribe — write under (agree to) transcribe — write across (copy) manuscript — hand-written inscription — written into

Group 4 self-check

Write your answer, then reveal to compare.

Q1
What is the key difference between conscription and voluntary enlistment?
Conscription is mandatory — the state requires you to serve. Voluntary enlistment is a personal choice. Conscription removes the choice.
Q2
Based on its root (scribere = to write), explain in your own words why "conscription" makes sense as a word for forced military service.
The government "writes your name together" with others on a military list. You didn't sign it yourself — it was written for you. The root captures the idea of being registered or recorded without consent.

Q1 — Definition match
Which word means "to make something less severe without eliminating it"?
Mitigate.
Q2 — Scenario
A famous chef gives a glowing public recommendation for a new kitchen appliance. Which word fits?
Endorse. The chef is publicly backing the product.
Q3 — Roots
Three words in this list share the Latin root vox (voice). Name them.
Equivocate, vociferous, and (as a cousin) advocate. Core two: equivocate and vociferous.
Q4 — Contrast
What is the difference between engender and detract? Use a single example situation to show both.
Engender = bring something into existence. Detract = reduce or take away from something. Example: a kind teacher engenders confidence in students; harsh criticism detracts from their motivation.
Q5 — Sentence writing
Write a sentence using "supersede" correctly.
Example: "The updated guidelines superseded the 2019 version, making it officially outdated." Key: something old is replaced entirely by something new.
Q6 — Scenario
A librarian is gathering articles from 12 journals, putting them in date order, and grouping them by topic. Which word describes what she is doing?
Collating. She is organizing and bringing together material from multiple sources.
Q7 — Precision
What does "denote" mean, and how is it different from "connote"? (Hint: connote was not on the list, but it shares the same root.)
Denote = the direct, literal meaning. Connote = the implied or emotional meaning. "Snake" denotes a reptile. It connotes danger or deception. Same root (notare = to mark), different prefix.
Q8 — Scenario
A country passes a law requiring all 18-year-olds to serve one year in the military. What is this policy called?
Conscription (or a conscription policy). It is mandatory, not voluntary.
Q9 — Contrast
How are "appraising" and "synopsis" related? How are they different?
Both involve making sense of information. A synopsis compresses content into a summary view. Appraising goes further — it assigns value or judgment. You might write a synopsis of a book, then appraise whether it is worth reading.
Q10 — Final challenge
Use any four words from the list in a short paragraph (4 to 6 sentences) about a topic of your choice. Underline the vocabulary words.
Model: "The school board remained sanguine despite growing opposition to the new attendance policy. Parents were vociferous at the meeting, demanding that the board endorse a more flexible approach. Critics argued that the rigid rules would engender anxiety in students rather than discipline. A revised policy was eventually released that mitigated the strictest requirements while preserving the core goals." — Words used: sanguine, vociferous, endorse, engender, mitigate.
