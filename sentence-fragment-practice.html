Is It a Standalone Sentence? — Digital SAT English Prep

Monica Winkler | Digital SAT English Prep
Step 1

Know the three possibilities

Every group of words you see on the SAT is one of three things:

Standalone sentence
Has a subject + a working verb. Can stand alone. A complete thought.
Dependent clause
Has a subject + a working verb, but begins with a word that makes it lean on another clause.
Fragment
Missing a subject, a working verb, or both. Cannot stand alone no matter what.
Standalone The committee approved the proposal.
Dependent Although the committee approved the proposal
Fragment The committee, having approved the proposal
Step 2

How to spot a dependent clause

Subordinating conjunctions make a clause dependent

A subordinating conjunction attached to the front of a subject-verb pair removes its independence. The most common ones on the SAT:

although, because, since, while, when, if, unless, after, before, even though, as, whereas, until, whether, once
Standalone The data supports the hypothesis.
Dependent Because the data supports the hypothesis

Adding because creates a clause that raises a question: so what? It needs a main clause to complete the thought.

Relative pronouns also create dependent clauses

Words like which, who, whose, that, and where introduce relative clauses that cannot stand alone.

Fragment The scientist who discovered the new element
This has a subject (scientist) but the relative clause who discovered the new element has swallowed the main verb slot. There is no main verb left for scientist to perform.
Step 3

The SAT's trickiest fragments

Infinitive phrases (starting with "to")

An infinitive (to run, to argue, to establish) is not a working verb. A sentence that uses only an infinitive has no real verb.

Fragment To improve air quality in densely populated urban centers
This looks impressive, but to improve is not a verb that tells us what a subject does. There is no subject performing an action.

Participial / gerund phrases (starting with -ing or -ed)

An -ing word used as a noun (gerund) or as a modifier (participle) is not a working verb either.

Fragment Studying the effects of deforestation on regional rainfall patterns
Studying here is a participle acting as a modifier, not a working verb. Ask: who is doing this? The sentence never tells us.
Quick test: Can you replace the underlined words with he/she/it does something or they do something and have it make sense as a complete sentence? If not, it is not a standalone sentence.
Strategy

Put it all together

  1. Find the main subject: who or what is the sentence about?
  2. Find the main verb: what does that subject do or what links it to a description? The verb must be a working verb, not an infinitive or a participle.
  3. Check the first word: is it a subordinating conjunction? If yes, the group is a dependent clause, not a standalone sentence.
  4. Watch for to + verb at the start: that is always a fragment.
  5. Watch for an -ing word with no helping verb (is running is fine; running alone is not).
  6. If subject and working verb are both present and no subordinating conjunction is at the front, the group is a standalone sentence.
