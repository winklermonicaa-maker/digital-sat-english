Five Steps to Answering Reading Questions
  • 1 Read the question.
  • 2 Identify the question type.
  • 3 Read the passage actively, looking for the answer.
  • 4 Underline the answer (or the evidence).
  • 5 Use process of elimination.
Detail Questions

What are they testing?

Detail questions test your ability to understand what the question is asking and to find the answer in the text quickly. There is no need to interpret anything: the answer is always directly in the passage.

According to the text Based on the text

Tips

Make sure you understand the question, then go straight to process of elimination.

Stick to the passage. The answer is there: no need to interpret anything beyond what is written.

Feel 100% confident before committing to an answer. Underline it in the passage first.

Process of Elimination: What to Watch Out For

  • OppositeThe answer choice says the exact opposite of what the passage says.
  • Not mentionedThe answer choice introduces something the passage never brought up.
  • Could be trueThe choice might make sense in the real world, but there is no evidence for it in the passage. Only what is in the passage world counts.
  • Exact wordsThe choice lifts words directly from the passage, which feels familiar, but those words are not answering the question. The correct answer is usually paraphrased.
  • Always / NeverEvery word in a choice must be supported by the text. Absolute language like always, never, and completely is almost never fully supported.
Central Idea Questions

What are they testing?

Central idea questions test your ability to identify the key message the author is getting across and to differentiate the main idea from the supporting details.

Main idea

Tips

Read the passage actively, noticing which topics keep coming up.

The main idea is often stated directly in the passage, usually in the first or last sentence. Ask yourself: which sentence do all the others relate to?

For fiction: when you finish reading, ask yourself, "what was that about?"

For non-fiction: the topic is what the passage is about. The main idea is what the author is saying about that topic. Those are not the same thing.

Key distinction: If the passage is about octopuses, "octopuses" is the topic. The main idea is the specific claim the author is making about them.

Process of Elimination: What to Watch Out For

  • OppositeThe choice states the exact opposite of what the passage says.
  • Not mentionedThe passage does not mention what is in the answer choice.
  • Half-trueHalf of the choice makes sense, but the other half does not. The whole answer needs to be correct.
  • Right answer, wrong questionThe choice sounds smart and is supported by the passage, but it describes a detail, not the main idea.
  • Exact wordsThe choice uses words from the passage, which feels familiar, but those words are not answering the question. The correct answer is usually paraphrased.

Detail Question

The following text is adapted from Kate Chopin's 1899 novel The Awakening. Edna Pontellier is staying with her husband at a resort in Louisiana.

It was eleven o'clock that night when Mr. Pontellier returned from Klein's hotel. He was in an excellent humor, in high spirits, and very talkative. His entrance awoke his wife, who was in bed and fast asleep when he came in. He talked to her while he undressed, telling her anecdotes and bits of news and gossip that he had gathered during the day. She was overcome with sleep, and answered him with little half utterances. He thought it very discouraging that his wife, who was the sole object of his existence, evinced so little interest in things which concerned him, and valued so little his conversation.
Kate Chopin, The Awakening (1899)
According to the text, why does Mr. Pontellier find his wife's behavior discouraging?
Answer Mr. Pontellier finds it discouraging that his wife shows little interest in what concerns him and does not value his conversation.
Where it is in the passage The final sentence states it directly: he felt discouraged because she "evinced so little interest in things which concerned him, and valued so little his conversation."
Self-check
Did your answer focus on her lack of interest in his conversation? If so, you are on track. A common trap here: she was asleep when he arrived, but that is not the reason for the discouragement. The issue is her response to him, not the timing of her sleep.
Passage 2 of 3
Main Idea Question

The following text is adapted from Charles Darwin's The Voyage of the Beagle (1839).

I was much interested, on several occasions, by watching the habits of an Octopus, or cuttle-fish. Although common in the pools of water left by the retiring tide, these animals were not easily caught. By means of their long arms and suckers, they could drag their bodies into very narrow crevices; and when thus fixed, it required great force to remove them. At other times they darted tail first, with the rapidity of an arrow, from one side of the pool to the other, at the same instant discolouring the water with a dark chestnut-brown ink. These animals also escape detection by a very extraordinary, chameleon-like power of changing their colour. They appear to vary their tints according to the nature of the ground over which they pass: when in deep water, their general shade was brownish purple, but when placed on the land, or in shallow water, this dark tint changed into one of a yellowish green.
Charles Darwin, The Voyage of the Beagle (1839)
What is the main idea of this passage? Write it in one sentence.
Answer Octopuses use several behaviors, including hiding in crevices, releasing ink, and changing color, to avoid being caught or detected.
What ties it together Every detail in the passage describes a different method the octopus uses to escape capture or detection. The topic is octopuses; the main idea is what Darwin is saying about them: that they have multiple effective defenses.
Self-check
Did your sentence capture multiple defenses or survival behaviors, not just one? A common "Right answer, wrong question" trap would be writing that the main idea is about color-changing: that is accurate but only covers one detail from the passage.
Passage 3 of 3
Detail Question

The following text is adapted from Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859).

When on board H.M.S. Beagle as naturalist, I was much struck with certain facts in the distribution of the organic beings inhabiting South America, and in the geological relations of the present to the past inhabitants of that continent. On my return home, it occurred to me, in 1837, that something might perhaps be made out on this question by patiently accumulating and reflecting on all sorts of facts which could possibly have any bearing on it. After five years' work I allowed myself to speculate on the subject, and drew up some short notes; these I enlarged in 1844 into a sketch of the conclusions which then seemed to me probable.
Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859)
According to the text, what prompted Darwin to begin investigating the origin of species?
Answer Darwin was prompted by observations he made during his voyage on the Beagle: specifically, facts about the distribution of living things in South America and their relationship to past inhabitants of the continent.
Where it is in the passage The first sentence says he "was much struck with certain facts" observed on the Beagle. The second sentence explains that these observations led him to begin investigating after his return.
Self-check
The key word is "prompted": your answer must come from the passage, not from what you already know about Darwin. Watch out for this trap: the passage says the observations were made during the voyage, not after returning home. An answer that says he "discovered new evidence after returning" misreads the sequence.
